##

It was in 1950 that Rudolph Carnap wrote Logical Foundations of Probability. In this book, he stated what has become known as the Principle of Total Evidence, one that says that when a rational agent is estimating the probability of a hypothesis, he must use all available relevant information.[^rudolph] This resonates with the Hippocratic principle of drawing inferences from all the signs (*sēmeia*, σημεῖα) taken together (*τεκμαίρεσθαι τοῖσι σύμπασι σημείοισιν*, *tekmaíresthai toîsi sýmpasi sēmeíoisin*).[^piazza][^most][^signs] When making a diagnostic inference, someone must start with something; this something takes then a epistemic role in the justification process.

This something, initially, takes the role of a sign (this something is *signified*) by a semiotic process. Following a Peircean conception of semiotics, something functions as a sign insofar as it enters into a triadic semiotic relation involving a sign, an object, and an interpretant.

The first objective step in diagnostic inference is to bring signs into the inferential process (i.e., they are transfered to the *space of reasons* to take a Sellarsian turn). For this to occur, signs must take the form of tekmêrion (τεκμήριον). Although the term already appears in the Hippocratic texts, it is further developed by Aristotle in the Rhetoric.[^rethoric] There, tekmêrion has the sense of a proof: a sign whose relation to what it signifies is such that it supports a necessary inference. Here, this word has the broader sense of a sign that serves an epistemic role in justification.

> Among signs, on the other hand, some are related as particular to universal, others as universal to particular. Among these, a necessary sign is a proof (*tekmêrion*), while that which is not necessary has no name indicating the difference. I call “necessary” those from which a deduction comes about.
>
> — Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, 1357b1.

Tekmerization is the epistemic-semiotic operation by which a sign is promoted to the status of an inferential candidate; that is, it comes to be regarded as a possible basis for determining something about its dynamic object. Tekmerization is primary when it is grounded in the interpreter’s own perceptual experience of the sign, and secondary when the interpreter’s access to that sign is mediated by another sign. Seeing a echymosis is different of someone saying to you that there's a echymosis. In the first case, error may arise within a single semiotic process of interpretation; in the second, there are two distinct opportunities for error: one in the original interpreter’s apprehension of the sign and another in the subsequent transmission or interpretation of that apprehension.

The error that occurs in the first semiotic process is here called a *perceptual error*, whereas an error arising in the second, testimonial process is called a *testimonial error*.

[^signs] The distinction between *sēmeion* (σημεῖον) and *tekmērion* (τεκμήριον) is considerably less rigid in the Hippocratic Corpus than it would later become in Aristotle. In Hippocratic medicine, the two terms occupy overlapping semantic fields and both may designate perceptible evidence from which the physician reasons about what is not directly observable; there appears to be no systematic distinction according to which a *tekmērion* is intrinsically more certain than a *sēmeion*. Indeed, the verbs *sēmainein* and *tekmairesthai* can themselves cross these lexical boundaries: one may *tekmairesthai* from *sēmeia*, as in the *Prognostic*, or *sēmainesthai* from *tekmēria*. Aristotle subsequently gives the terminology a sharper logical structure, treating *tekmērion* as a particular kind of *sēmeion*: a necessary or incontestable sign capable of grounding a conclusive inference. The Aristotelian distinction should therefore not be straightforwardly projected back onto Hippocratic usage.
