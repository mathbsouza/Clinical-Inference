## The Common Script

A patient presents with a constellation of symptoms and signs. Normally, if he doesn't have any diagnosis, it is expected that, if the symptom is not associated with any specialty, for example epigastric pain and gastroenterology, it is the role of Internal Medicine to assume the case. The patient is then investigated, and, if a diagnosis is reached, the patient is referred to a certain specialty for follow-up.

This is the "common" script that plays out in Medicine today.

But is Internal Medicine the appropriate specialty for this? The definition of Internal Medicine is commonly given as "the medical specialty devoted to the comprehensive care of adult patients, focused in the diagnosis and non-surgical treatment of diseases affecting internal organs and systems, excluding gyneco-obstetrical problems, and the prevention of those diseases."[^reyes2006] It is not solely focused on the diagnosis of diseases. The act of diagnosis is considered to permeate all medical specialties. It is not something any single specialty can claim as its own.

Here, I argue that the concept of diagnosis should map onto a specialty, and this specialty, as of today, does not exist yet. We are talking about the role of the diagnostician as such in a world where diagnosis stops being something intuitive, almost an "art", and starts to be a systematic realm of study within Evidence-Based Diagnosis and Philosophy of Diagnosis. We can't rely solely on romantic notions of evidence, clinical semiology, and intuitive clinical inference; we must embrace diagnostics as a science in itself.[^alencar-borin2025]

## The Motivation: We Have A LOT of Diagnostic Errors

If we make a diagnosis and this diagnosis is not justified, we incur a diagnostic error. There is no consensus on what constitutes a diagnostic error, and we will talk about it in another article. However, in my opinion, it is closely related to this question: what was justified to think in a given diagnostic context? A right diagnosis can be justified; a wrong diagnosis can also be justified. What characterizes a diagnostic error is not the relationship between our belief and the real world, but how we justify this belief.

The burden of diagnostic errors in the US is very significant. The US National Academy of Medicine says that it is a "moral, professional, and public health imperative" to improve diagnosis in healthcare. To see why, let's look at some data.

- In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies with more than 80,000 hospitalized patients, harmful diagnostic errors occurred in approximately 0.7% of adult admissions, translating to approximately 250,000 harmful inpatient diagnostic errors per year in the US. The most commonly missed diagnoses were malignancy, at 11%, and pulmonary embolism, at 9.6%.[^gunderson]

- Diagnostic errors cause approximately 795,000 deaths and permanent incapacities every year. Just 15 diseases, led by stroke, sepsis, pneumonia, VTE, and lung cancer, account for about half of this harm.[^newman-toker2023]

- Doctors in general suck at estimating pre-test probabilities, which impacts post-test probabilities in a nonlinear way. Bayesianism is not "natural" in a formal way to most clinicians. Because the relationship between pre-test and post-test probability is nonlinear, even relatively small errors in the initial probability estimate may substantially distort the interpretation of subsequent diagnostic evidence.[^souza-alencar-pretest]

- Diagnostic error is not confined to hospitalized patients. An estimated 5.08% of US adults experience a diagnostic error in outpatient care each year — approximately 12 million people — and about half of these errors may be potentially harmful.[^singh2014]

- Diagnostic errors are often failures of inference rather than simple failures of knowledge. In a study of 100 diagnostic errors in internal medicine, cognitive factors contributed to 74% of cases and system-related factors to 65%. Faulty synthesis and premature closure were prominent cognitive causes, whereas inadequate medical knowledge was relatively uncommon.[^graber2005]

- Confidence is also poorly calibrated to diagnostic accuracy. In a vignette study of 118 internists, physicians correctly diagnosed 55.3% of easier cases but only 5.8% of difficult ones, while their mean confidence decreased only from 7.2 to 6.4 out of 10. In other words, diagnostic difficulty produced a dramatic fall in accuracy without a comparable fall in confidence.[^meyer2013]

This data show us that there's a problem in how we deal with diagnosis today. We treat it as something accidental, not someting to be mastered and apprimored.

## The Hidden Gap of Diagnosis Today

Diagnosis has become a sufficiently complex body of knowledge that it can no longer be adequately represented merely as a distributed competence across medical specialties. Today, we tend to assume that all physicians are capable of diagnosis — that is, that they possess the cognitive abilities and training required to determine what condition a patient has. Of course, they do not. The problem is that, in addition to the complexity of the human body, diagnosis is itself an *epistemically* complex activity, and not every differential diagnosis falls neatly within the boundaries of a single specialty. Cough, for example, may have cardiac, respiratory, neurological, or many other kinds of causes. Why do we assume that every doctor should possess the knowledge necessary to differentiate among them?

A cardiologist may have extensive knowledge of the cardiac causes of cough, and a pulmonologist of its respiratory causes. Yet, even if they work together throughout the diagnostic process (for example, by referring the patient from one specialty to another), they may still lack the specific knowledge required to determine what makes a pulmonary diagnosis more probable than a cardiac one. Before we know that the cough is cardiac or pulmonary, someone must determine which of these possibilities deserves serious consideration in the first place.

This creates a gap in the way medicine is organized today. Many medical specialties are defined, at least partly, by the class of diseases they treat—cardiology deals with cardiovascular disease, neurology with disorders of the nervous system, and gastroenterology with diseases of the gastrointestinal tract.[^exception] However, before a patient can be assigned to a particular disease within a particular physiological domain, a diagnosis must first be established. In other words, many specialties are organized around what a disease turns out to be, while no specialty is primarily organized around the general problem of determining what the disease is the case.

Internal Medicine only partially can claim this place, but only in an imperfect mode. Today, internists are generalists, and, although diagnostic reasoning is central to their practice, diagnosis is only one among many of their responsibilities. They also provide longitudinal care, manage chronic and acute disease, coordinate treatment, prevent illness, and care for patients with multimorbidity. Diagnostic expertise therefore remains embedded within a broader specialty rather than being treated as an independent object of specialized knowledge.

The gap, therefore, is not simply that some physicians are better diagnosticians than others. The deeper gap is institutional. Medicine has created specialties for increasingly narrow domains of disease, but it has not created a corresponding specialty for the domain-general problem that precedes them: how to determine, in a systematic and justified way, what disease a patient has.[^diagnostic-gap] This gap becomes increasingly important as diagnosis itself becomes a more explicit object of scientific study.[^diagnosis-field] For example, Evidence-Based Diagnosis provides formal tools for estimating and updating diagnostic probabilities.[^evidence-based-diagnosis] At the same time, research on diagnostic practice (clinical reasoning and probabilistic inference) increasingly treats the diagnostic process as an object of investigation in its own right.[^diagnostic-research] What we might call Diagnostic Theory --- a general theory of how diagnostic hypotheses should be generated, compared, updated, justified, and ultimately accepted or rejected --- could therefore provide the theoretical foundation for such a specialty.

## The Theory Being Constructed

If we want a specialty to claim diagnosis as its own, it must have a *theoretical corpus* — a science of diagnostics.[^aristotle-corpus] [^diagnosis-laor] Such a theoretical corpus is something that must be constructed. It should consist of two pillars: one ontological and one epistemic.

* **The Pillar of Nosology**: A diagnostician should know how diseases are constructed, defined, and situated within a nosology. We should know *what there is* — that is, what is assumed in our ontology. Even if the diagnostician does not possess the full pathophysiological knowledge relevant to a given disease, which properly belongs to the specialty concerned with that disease, he should understand how the disease entity, qua classification, ought to be situated within the diagnostic process.

* **The Pillar of Diagnostic Inference**: Not only should he know the diseases he uses to classify patients; he must also have a sound methodology for connecting these labels to individual patients. He must have some theory of how diagnostic inference should be performed as efficiently as possible. To this end, he must draw on Bayesian epistemology, information theory, the theory of signs, and related fields in order to construct an epistemic account of how perceptual phenomena, as apprehended by the physician, can be transformed into data within the space of reasons that constitutes diagnostic inference.

Both pillars are already present in the literature, but in an unsystematic and fragmented way The field of nosology, for example, includes debates about the definition and existence of diseases (boundaries of syndromes and disease categories), systems of disease classification (the organization of entities within systems such as the ICD) and questions of ontological nosology (debates over whether diseases are natural kinds or pragmatic constructs). The second pillar appears in the literature on diagnostic reasoning, Bayesian reasoning, and artificial intelligence, particularly in attempts to model diagnostic inference. Within a science of diagnostics, one may additionally distinguish between **empirical diagnostics**, concerned with *how physicians actually reason* — for example, the study of heuristics used in diagnostic inference, cognitive biases, illness scripts, and expert pattern recognition — and **theoretical diagnostics**, concerned with *how they ought to reason* — for example, how evidence should update diagnostic probabilities, how competing hypotheses should be compared, and how diagnostic tests should be selected under conditions of uncertainty.

## We Are Paving the Roadway

We have seen that diagnostic error is a real clinical problem — it's not something to be ignored. It imposes substantial burdens on healthcare and patients and, therefore, we should now examine it more closely. Medical specialties think that they master this art, but only in a rudimentary (and wrong) form. The emergence of Evidence-Based Diagnosis and the literature about diagnostic reasoning appears at a time that makes it clear that we can no longer treat diagnosis in an unsystematic way. Along these lines, it becomes clear that a science of diagnostics is not optional; it is a necessity. We should organize and develop such a science so that it can serve as the foundation for a new kind of specialty — or subspecialty — whose practitioners master this domain: the art of diagnosis or the science of diagnostics. Only in this way can we treat diagnosis as what it really is: a complex epistemic activity that belongs to its proper domain.

[^exception]: There are important exceptions to this disease- or organ-based organization of medical specialties. Palliative Care is organized around a particular form and goal of care rather than around a class of diseases; Emergency Medicine around a particular clinical setting and mode of presentation; Critical Care around physiological severity; Geriatric Medicine around a patient population; and Preventive Medicine around a particular medical objective. Other specialties, such as Anesthesiology, Radiology, and Pathology, are organized primarily around particular functions, methods, and/or technologies. Yet none of them takes diagnosis itself as its primary object. Radiology and Pathology provide indispensable diagnostic information, Emergency Medicine and Internal Medicine routinely solve diagnostic problems, and virtually every medical specialty engages in diagnosis within its own domain; nevertheless, none is organized around the general problem of determining, across domains, what disease or condition best explains a patient's presentation. In this sense, diagnosis remains a function performed throughout medicine without being the defining object of any medical specialty.
