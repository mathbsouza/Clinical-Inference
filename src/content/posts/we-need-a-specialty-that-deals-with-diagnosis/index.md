A patient presents with a constellation of symptoms and signs. Normally, if he doesn't have any diagnosis, it is expected that, if the symptom is not associated with any specialty, for example epigastric pain and gastroenterology, it is the role of Internal Medicine to assume the case. The patient is then investigated, and, if a diagnosis is reached, the patient is referred to a certain specialty for follow-up.

This is the "common" script that plays out in Medicine today.

But is Internal Medicine the appropriate specialty for this? The definition of Internal Medicine is commonly given as "the medical specialty devoted to the comprehensive care of adult patients, focused in the diagnosis and non-surgical treatment of diseases affecting internal organs and systems, excluding gyneco-obstetrical problems, and the prevention of those diseases."[^reyes2006] It is not solely focused on the diagnosis of diseases. The act of diagnosis is considered to permeate all medical specialties. It is not something any single specialty can claim as its own.

Here, I argue that, analogous to the relationship between the concept of palliative care and the specialty of palliative care, the concept of diagnosis should map onto a specialty, and this specialty, as of today, does not exist yet. We are talking about the role of the diagnostician as such in a world where diagnosis stops being something intuitive, almost an "art", and starts to be a systematic realm of study within Evidence-Based Diagnosis and Philosophy of Diagnosis. We can't rely solely on romantic notions of evidence, clinical semiology, and intuitive clinical inference; we must embrace diagnostics as a science in itself.[^alencar-borin2025]

## The Motivation: We Have A LOT of Diagnostic Errors

The burden of diagnostic errors in the US is very significant. The US National Academy of Medicine says that it is a "moral, professional, and public health imperative" to improve diagnosis in healthcare. To see why, let's look at some data.

In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies with more than 80,000 hospitalized patients, harmful diagnostic errors occurred in approximately 0.7% of adult admissions, translating to approximately 250,000 harmful inpatient diagnostic errors per year in the US. The most commonly missed diagnoses were malignancy, at 11%, and pulmonary embolism, at 9.6%.

Diagnostic errors cause approximately 795,000 deaths and permanent incapacities every year. Just 15 diseases, led by stroke, sepsis, pneumonia, VTE, and lung cancer, account for about half of this harm.[^newman-toker2023]

Doctors in general suck at estimating pre-test probabilities, which impacts post-test probabilities in a nonlinear way. Bayesianism is not "natural" in a formal way to most clinicians.[^souza-alencar-pretest]

If we make a diagnosis and this diagnosis is not justified, we incur a diagnostic error. There is no consensus on what constitutes a diagnostic error, and we will talk about it in another article, but, in my opinion, it is closely related to this question: what was justified to think in a given diagnostic context? A right diagnosis can be justified; a wrong diagnosis can also be justified. What characterizes a diagnostic error is not the relationship between our belief and the real world, but how we justify this belief.

## The Theory Being Constructed

If we want some specialty to claim diagnosis as its own, it must have a theoretical corpus. This theoretical corpus is something that has been constructed. This corpus should consist of three pillars.

### The Pillar of Nosology

A diagnostician should know how diseases are constructed, defined, and placed within a nosology. Even if it lacks the physiopathological specifications, which are the concern of the specialty that deals with the disease in question, a diagnostician should focus on how the entity, as a classification, should be placed in the process of diagnosis.

### The Pillar of Bayesian Epistemology and Methods of Classification

Not only should he know the diseases that he uses for classifying patients, he must also have a good methodology for connecting these labels to patients.
