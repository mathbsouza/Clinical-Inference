## The Evidence-Based Medicine

The relationship between evidence, medicine, and justification is aggregated in what has been called "Evidence-Based Medicine" (EBM). Evidence-Based Medicine, as defined by Guyatt, Sackett, Straus, and many others, consists in "the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients."[^1] The word "conscientious" here can mean many things, but one of them is the belief that a doctor D is justified in believing B only if he has some kind of cognitive access to the evidence that "grounds" his belief that B.

For example, if I think that some pril-drug is a good drug for treating hypertension in a patient, then I must have some evidence E-pril-drug that correctly concludes, that is, there is an appropriate methodology for it, that "this pril-drug has some good magnitude of effect for treating hypertension in this population," and the doctor D knows this E-pril-drug. Of course, this instance of evidentialism, a strong form of it, can appear reasonable at first. After all, we all think that, in an ideal world based on Evidence-Based Medicine, every doctor must ground their conduct in evidence.

## The Problem of Internal Evidentialism

What we mean by evidence is not a solid ground in Evidence-Based Medicine.[^2] [^3] However, even if it is not a solid ground, we can, despite this problem, talk about it in intuitive terms: evidence is something that justifies our beliefs.

We can discuss whether some piece of evidence is appropriate for one conduct or another, but if what we call evidence does not justify anything, it is very hard to say that it is evidence for something after all. So, evidence is closely related to the process of justification. The evidentialist position tries to make the process of justification closely related to the empirical world, but not only the empirical world. Someone can justify their position through the process of deduction: a conclusion following from reasonable premises.

For example: we know that there is evidence that a treatment A makes something true; we know that this something is associated with better outcomes, and the association is grounded in good mechanistic reasoning. Would it be reasonable to conclude that A therefore leads to better outcomes? I think that this is the case in some instances, where the magnitude of the effect is so evident that bias is the most improbable explanation.[^4]

However, the main point of this text is not evidence in itself. It is the relationship between us and the evidence within the evidentialist position. There are two ramifications that can appear within it. One of them is the demand that we must have cognitive access to the evidence that justifies our belief. The other is the negation of this thesis: we can be justified even if we do not have cognitive access to it.

The problem with the first position, here called internal evidentialism, is that it imposes too much constraint on clinical practice. Imagine, for example, some doctor A who reads the primary evidence that grounds his practice. For instance, if he prescribes some pril-drug, he knows the evidence that justifies its prescription. The other is doctor B, who only does what others have told him, reliably, is the case. He has grounds to think that others have a good ability to analyze evidence.

Even if there is no direct connection between B and the evidence, is he justified in his conduct?

I think that he is not only justified, but that there are also solid reasons to think that he is justified. He may also have better reasons to believe that he is more likely to be correct than A, even without direct awareness of the primary evidence. The reason for this is what is called, in the literature of social epistemology, Epistemic Dependence.[^5] For the case where A is not a specialist, it is very straightforward to see why B must be better informed: he is informed by specialists. But even in the case where A is indeed a specialist, B is also better informed.

## Why Is B Better Informed?

Let us suppose that, for some matter, A has a probability of 0.9 of being correct in his evaluation of the evidence, of all evidence, and not only comparative evidence. Let us suppose that specialists each have a 0.75 probability of being correct. Suppose that their judgments are independent and that the collective conclusion is determined by a simple majority rule. According to the Condorcet Jury Theorem,[^6] because each specialist is more likely to be correct than incorrect, if we sufficiently increase the number of specialists, the chance of the collection of specialists being right becomes greater than the chance of A being right. In this specific case, a number of 7 specialists would be necessary to have a confidence greater than 0.9.[^7]

Of course, we expect specialists to have a greater capacity for interpreting the evidence correctly, approximately that of a single individual, and therefore, it is unreasonable to think that one individual is better than a group of specialists.

## The Function of Guidelines

This reveals to us an important epistemic function of guidelines. Guidelines were created because not every doctor can critically appraise the primary evidence. But even if they could, they are not better than a group of specialists evaluating the evidence. Specialists can be wrong, that is how science works, but it is very improbable that they are wrong all the time. This does not mean, however, that we cannot disagree, but rather that we do so with epistemic humility.

Evidence-Based Medicine is an institutional practice, and not an individual practice. The process of justification is social, not individual. We can have epistemic justification even if unaware of the things that make our conducts justified. That's where Epistemic Dependence has its role. Individuality has its part, but is secondary. That's why the pyramid of acquisition of evidence starts with summaries, synopses of syntheses, synthesis, and etc. The primary evidence is on the bottom, not on the top.
